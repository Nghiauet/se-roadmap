<Lead>
Microservices solve **organisational** problems, not technical ones. Splitting a service
costs you transactions, atomicity, and easy debugging. You should only pay that cost
when you're getting team-scaling or independent-deploy benefits in return. The modular
monolith is the right answer for years.
</Lead>

## The big idea

The "monolith vs microservices" axis is really two axes:

  leftTitle="Code organisation"
  rightTitle="Deployment unit"
  rows={[

    { attribute: 'Modular monolith', left: 'Strict module boundaries', right: 'One process' },

The interesting move is from **big ball of mud → modular monolith**. Splitting into
processes is the *second* step — only when modular boundaries aren't enough.

## What microservices actually buy you

<Steps>
  <Step title="Independent deployability">
    Team A ships without waiting for Team B's release branch.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Independent scaling">
    The image-resizer needs 50 instances; the auth service needs 3.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Technology heterogeneity">
    The new ML service can be Python while everything else is Go.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Failure isolation">
    A bug in the recommendation engine doesn't take down checkout.
  </Step>
</Steps>

All four are mostly **about teams**. If you're one team of five, you're paying the cost
without getting the benefit.

## What you pay for

Microservices replace function calls with network calls. The bill:

<DefinitionList>
  <Definition term="Distributed transactions">Two services, one user action. No global commit. You need sagas or careful eventual consistency.</Definition>
  <Definition term="Network failure">Calls timeout, fail, retry. Every boundary gains circuit breakers and idempotency keys.</Definition>
  <Definition term="Observability">"What happened in this request?" becomes correlating across N services. Distributed tracing is non-negotiable.</Definition>
  <Definition term="Operational overhead">N services × M environments = service catalogue, dashboards, CI/CD pipelines, on-call rotations.</Definition>
  <Definition term="Local dev">Running 30 services on a laptop is its own engineering project.</Definition>
</DefinitionList>

## The modular monolith — the middle ground

A single process, but with **enforced module boundaries**: each module exposes only its
public API (interfaces, ports), persists into its own schema/tables, owns its tests.
Inter-module calls go through those public APIs only.

Why this is great:
- **Atomic transactions across modules** are still trivial — same DB.
- **Refactoring** is a compile-checked operation, not a contract negotiation.
- **Easy to split later**: a clean module boundary becomes a service boundary on demand.

## When to actually split

Split a module out when you have **at least two** of these signals:

- The module has its own scaling profile (10× or more different from the rest).
- An independent team owns it and the shared-deploy cycle is friction.
- It has a different technology fit (latency-sensitive ML, real-time streaming).
- The bug-tolerance is different (this one *must* keep working).

If you have only one of these, fix the underlying problem (separate the deploy pipeline,
profile the hot path) before adding a network boundary.

## A practical migration path

<Steps>
  <Step title="Find a seam in the monolith">
    A module already loosely-coupled, used through a small interface.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Extract a private API">
    First in-process: replace direct DB access with calls to a module facade.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Move the data">
    Migrate that module's tables into its own schema; the facade now owns them.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Add the network hop">
    Replace the in-process facade with an HTTP/gRPC client of the same shape; deploy the
    new service.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Add the resilience plumbing">
    Timeouts, retries, circuit breaker, tracing — for this one call.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## In practice

The teams that succeed with microservices usually went through years of modular monolith
first. The teams that fail with microservices usually skipped that step. **Start with a
modular monolith; split when the pain forces you.**
